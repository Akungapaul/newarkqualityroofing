## 0. GLOBAL CORRECTIONS — read before writing the page

These override looser phrasings and marketing instincts. Carry verbatim. Every rule
here is a critic-confirmed correction or a load-bearing caution from both researchers.

1. **NQR is a roofing CONTRACTOR — not a preservation architect, not the SHPO/HPO,
   not the Historic Preservation Commission, and not a tax advisor.** Position NQR as
   a roofing contractor experienced with historic/period roofing materials
   (slate, clay/terra-cotta tile, wood/cedar shingle, metal) that works **within** the
   Secretary of the Interior's Standards and **coordinates with** the owner's architect,
   the municipal Historic Preservation Commission, and the NJ DEP Historic Preservation
   Office. Frame every regulatory statement as "per the NPS / per the municipal Historic
   Preservation Commission / per the NJ DEP HPO / per the IRS" — NQR never approves,
   certifies, or determines historic status or credit eligibility.

2. **No "certified historic restoration contractor" / "preservation-certified"
   credential claim — CUT in any form.** No universal federal or state certification
   brands a roofer a "certified historic restoration contractor." NQR may state
   demonstrable experience with slate/tile/wood/metal historic roofs and adherence to
   the NPS Preservation Briefs and the Secretary's Standards. A specific
   manufacturer/material installer certification may be named **only if NQR actually
   holds it and it is true.**

3. **National Register or NJ Register LISTING alone does NOT bar or restrict a
   private-funded reroof — CUT any copy implying it does.** Verbatim (NPS):
   "Listing in the National Register places no federal restrictions or requirements on
   a private property owner." Verbatim (NJ HPO): "Listing in the New Jersey or National
   Registers of Historic Places does not place restrictions on private property owner
   rights." The **binding review gate** for a private reroof is whether the property is
   a **designated landmark or in a LOCAL historic district under a municipal ordinance**
   (which requires a **Certificate of Appropriateness** from the Historic Preservation
   Commission) — or whether the project uses federal/state funds, permits, or licenses.
   Do NOT write "because your home is on the National Register you cannot replace the
   roof without approval."

4. **In-kind / matching is the GOVERNING preservation principle.** Standard 6
   (verbatim): "Deteriorated historic features will be repaired rather than replaced.
   Where the severity of deterioration requires replacement of a distinctive feature,
   the new feature will match the old in design, color, texture and, where possible,
   materials." On a visible/character-defining roof, the historic material is repaired
   or replaced **in kind** — do NOT present asphalt shingle as a comparable substitute
   for a slate or clay-tile roof. Substitute (modern) materials are appropriate mainly
   on **flat or non-visible** roofs (Brief 4).

5. **The federal 20% Historic Rehabilitation Tax Credit (IRC §47) is
   INCOME-PRODUCING-ONLY and is a DIFFERENT, still-active credit from the repealed
   energy credits.** §47 applies only to depreciable (income-producing) certified
   historic structures — **owner-occupied homes do not qualify** (claimed ratably over
   5 years; NPS/NJ HPO: "Owner-occupied residential properties explicitly do not
   qualify"). It was **NOT repealed** by the 2025 budget law (One Big Beautiful Bill
   Act, P.L. 119-21). The §25D residential solar credit and §25C efficiency credit
   were terminated after Dec 31, 2025 by that same law — those are **separate** credits.
   Never imply "historic tax credits were repealed in 2025." Route a homeowner to a tax
   professional, NPS, and (for state) NJEDA — NQR does not assess credit eligibility.

6. **The enacted NJ historic tax credit (Historic Property Reinvestment Program / HPRP,
   NJEDA) is also INCOME-PRODUCING-ONLY** — residential projects must be rental with
   **at least four dwelling units**; owner-occupied homes are not eligible. Frame these
   credits as relevant to **commercial / income-producing / multi-family** historic
   buildings, not a homeowner's house.

7. **The PENDING NJ homeowner credit (S3545) is NOT law — do NOT present it as
   available.** NJ S3545 "Homeowners' Historic Property Reinvestment Act" (25% rate,
   $25,000-per-property cap, principal-residence occupancy) was introduced Feb 19, 2026
   and is **pending in committee** as of June 2026. The "refundable" and "over 10 years"
   qualifiers were **NOT verified** — drop them. Do not tell a homeowner this credit
   exists for them today.

8. **Treat all tax-credit caps and percentages as TIME-SENSITIVE 2026 program terms,
   not durable law.** The HPRP 50%/$8M, 60%/$12M, 45%/$50M figures and the aggregate
   $50M/yr–$300M/6yr program cap are set by P.L. 2020 c.156 as amended by P.L. 2024 c.61
   and NJEDA rules that were being amended into early 2026. The aggregate $50M/yr–$300M
   cap is a **SECONDARY-named** news-summary figure — do NOT state it as NJEDA-confirmed.

9. **Fastener metal is matched PER-MATERIAL — never generalized to "always use copper
   nails."** Slate and clay tile **require** non-ferrous (solid copper or stainless
   steel) fasteners — plain/galvanized steel "will usually rust out long before the
   slate itself begins to deteriorate" (Brief 29). But **red cedar must NOT use copper
   nails** — a chemical reaction between the wood and copper shortens the roof's life;
   use hot-dipped zinc-coated, aluminum, or stainless steel (Brief 19). This is a real
   material-specific split, not an error — do not collapse it.

10. **Do not walk on slate or high-profile clay tile, do not coat/seal/power-wash
    historic slate, and do not discard repairable original material.** "Slate roofs
    should not be walked on if at all possible" (Brief 29); "Clay roofing tiles are
    extremely fragile and cannot be walked on" (Brief 30). Coating/sealing slate to
    "seal out moisture" has historically made problems worse (Brief 29). Salvageable
    slates/tiles are sounded, flipped, and reused (Standard 6).

11. **A Certificate of Appropriateness (COA) does NOT replace a building permit, and
    vice versa.** They are separate approvals: a COA is historic design review from the
    HPC; a construction permit under the NJ Uniform Construction Code is separate. A
    reroof in a local district commonly needs both. Do not say a parcel needs a COA
    without confirming it is a designated landmark or in the designated LOCAL district
    (Register boundary ≠ local-ordinance boundary in every case).

12. **No precise cost figures in this pack.** This is a regulatory + technique pack;
    zero $/sq-ft or project-cost ranges for historic slate/clay/copper/wood reroofing
    were researched here. Any on-page price must come from `facts-materials-economics.md`
    (§2 slate, §5 wood, §6 tile, §7 NJ regional) with its own named source — do not
    invent a "historic slate restoration costs $X" figure.

## 3. Slate — NPS Preservation Brief 29 (Jeffrey S. Levine)

> Lifespan numbers cross-ref `facts-materials-economics.md` §2 (InterNACHI 60–150;
> National Slate Association premium 100+). Brief 29 adds the **preservation-grade**
> framing and the repair-method facts below.

| Claim | Value | Named source | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slate roofs are repaired rather than replaced whenever possible | Pattern, detailing, and craftsmanship are important design elements; "they should be repaired rather than replaced whenever possible." | NPS Preservation Brief 29 | PRIMARY |
| Slate lifespan / durability (preservation framing) | Properly installed slate "will last 60 to 125 years or longer"; some over 200 years. By type: Vermont/New York ~125 yr; Buckingham Virginia 175+ yr; Pennsylvania Soft-Vein 60+ yr (PA Hard-Vein ~100 yr; Monson, Maine ~200+ yr historically) | NPS Preservation Brief 29 | PRIMARY |
| Slate durability (trade-body cross-reference) | ASTM S-1 grade slate carries a minimum ~75-year life expectancy; many roofs perform well over 100 years and up to ~150 years, some over 200 | National Slate Association | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **Slate fasteners MUST be non-ferrous (copper or stainless), not plain/galvanized steel** | Verbatim: "Non-ferrous slater's nails, such as solid copper or stainless steel, should always be used since plain steel and galvanized nails will usually rust out long before the slate itself begins to deteriorate." Nails are NOT driven tight (the slate hangs on the shank); exposed nail heads accelerate deterioration of the overlying slate. | NPS Preservation Brief 29 | PRIMARY |
| Individual slates are replaced with a ripper + copper strip/hook (not mastic) | A broken slate's nails are cut/pulled with a "ripper"; the replacement is secured with a copper strip/tab nailed to the sheathing above the lower course, or with metal hooks/clips/straps bent over the tail. | Repair-vs-full-replacement threshold for slate | Verbatim: "If 20% or more of the slates on a roof or roof slope are broken, cracked, missing, or sliding out of position, it is usually less expensive to replace the roof than to execute individual repairs." Below ~20%, selective repair/replacement is preferred. | NPS Preservation Brief 29 | PRIMARY |
| Slate is not walked on; flashing matched to slate's life | Verbatim: "Slate roofs should not be walked on if at all possible." Where unavoidable, use ladders/boards to distribute weight, soft-soled shoes, and step on lower slate edges. Flashing should be a durable metal with a life comparable to the slate — copper, or lead-coated copper / terne-coated stainless steel (TCS). | NPS Preservation Brief 29 | PRIMARY |
| Do not coat/seal/paint historic slate | Slate should not be coated or painted; sealing to "seal out moisture" has historically exacerbated problems. Sound slate is unaffected by gentle washing; aggressive cleaning/coatings damage historic material. | NPS Preservation Brief 29 | PRIMARY |
- **Banned:** quoting any single number as THE slate lifespan (present the sourced
  range); plain/galvanized steel nails on slate; coating/sealing/power-washing slate as
  a repair; walking on slate; replacing a significant slate roof with asphalt shingles;
  cert claims.

## 4. Clay tile — NPS Preservation Brief 30 (Anne E. Grimmer & Paul K. Williams)

> Lifespan numbers cross-ref `facts-materials-economics.md` §6 (InterNACHI 100+;
> TRI Alliance clay 75–100+, concrete 40–75; underlayment is the real limiter).
> Brief 30 adds the preservation framing and repair-method facts.

| Claim | Value | Named source | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clay tile durability (preservation framing) | "Clay tile has one of the longest life expectancies among historic roofing materials — generally about 100 years." Tile frequently outlasts its fasteners/sheathing, so most failures stem from breakdown of fasteners, flashing, or sheathing rather than the tile itself. | NPS Preservation Brief 30 | PRIMARY |
| Clay tile must be matched in profile, color, glaze, and texture | Replacement tiles must match shape/profile, color, surface texture, and glaze (glazed vs unglazed natural terra cotta). Terra-cotta red is the most common color; subtle natural color variation and the exact profile (Spanish, Mission/barrel, pantile, flat/English shingle) are character-defining and must be reproduced. | NPS Preservation Brief 30 | PRIMARY |
| Clay tiles are fragile and cannot be walked on directly | Verbatim: "Clay roofing tiles are extremely fragile and cannot be walked on." High-profile pantiles "are very fragile and easily broken"; flat shingle tiles carry less risk for a careful roofer. Use plywood, planks, or burlap bags filled with material to distribute weight, and hook a ladder over the ridge. The hardest part of replacing one broken tile is doing so "without breaking neighboring tiles." | NPS Preservation Brief 30 | PRIMARY |
| Clay tile fasteners and flashing should be copper / non-ferrous | Tiles fastened with copper nails or hangers; gutters, flashings, and valleys (copper or lead) installed before laying tile. | Clay tile durability / underlayment limiter (trade cross-reference) | Clay often 75+ yr, many 100+; underlayment typically fails before the tile and is often the true repair-vs-replace trigger | Tile Roofing Industry Alliance (TRI Alliance) | PRIMARY-attrib |
| TRI Alliance is the named authority for clay/concrete tile installation standards | North American trade/technical body for clay and concrete roof tile; publishes installation manuals/standards used by contractors and code officials | Tile Roofing Industry Alliance | PRIMARY-attrib |
- **Banned:** walking on high-profile clay tile; iron/ferrous nails on clay tile; the
  stale "tile 50+" figure (the live InterNACHI chart is Clay/Concrete 100+); cert claims.
