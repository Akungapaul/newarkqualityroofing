## 0. GLOBAL CORRECTIONS — read before writing the page

These override looser phrasings and marketing instincts. Carry verbatim. Every rule
here is a critic-confirmed correction or a load-bearing compliance caution from both
researchers. The first three are LEGAL-EXPOSURE rules — a violation is not just an SEO
problem, it can be unlicensed public adjusting (N.J.S.A. 17:22B) or consumer/insurance
fraud (N.J.S.A. 56:8 / 17:33A) in New Jersey.

1. **NQR is a roofing CONTRACTOR — NOT a licensed public adjuster.** In New Jersey only
   a licensed **public adjuster** (Public Adjusters' Licensing Act, **N.J.S.A. 17:22B**,
   administered by **NJ DOBI**) — or a licensed attorney — may, for a fee, investigate,
   negotiate, adjust, or settle a first-party property-insurance claim **on behalf of the
   insured**. N.J.S.A. 17:22B-3: "no adjuster shall act on behalf of an insured unless
   licensed as a public adjuster." NQR **DOES**: inspect the roof, document storm / hail /
   fire / leak damage with photos, prepare a detailed written **scope and estimate**, meet
   the insurer's adjuster **on site** to walk the damage, and perform the **approved work**.
   NQR must **NOT**: "handle / file / manage your claim," "negotiate or settle with your
   insurer," "maximize your settlement," "deal with the adjuster on your behalf," interpret
   your policy / tell you what's covered, or use percentage-of-claim / contingency pricing.
   Compliant: "The homeowner (or a licensed public adjuster) files and negotiates the claim;
   we provide the roofing documentation and do the roofing."

2. **NEVER waive / cover / eat / absorb / rebate / credit the deductible, and NEVER advertise
   a "free roof" or "no out-of-pocket" replacement.** The deductible is the policyholder's
   legal responsibility and is subtracted once from the covered loss. New Jersey has no
   Texas/Colorado-style contractor-deductible statute, but a deductible-waiver scheme
   (typically inflating the estimate so the insurer's overpayment covers the deductible) is
   prosecutable under the **NJ Consumer Fraud Act (N.J.S.A. 56:8)** and may implicate the
   **NJ Insurance Fraud Prevention Act (N.J.S.A. 17:33A)**. Compliant: "Your deductible is
   your responsibility under your policy; we cannot legally waive or pay it."

3. **NEVER guarantee, promise, or "ensure" the claim — or a full replacement over a repair —
   will be approved, or paid a specific dollar amount.** Coverage and approval are the
   insurer's decision. No "we get claims approved" / "leader in claim approval." Compliant:
   "We document the damage thoroughly so you and your insurer can evaluate the claim; we
   cannot guarantee any coverage outcome."

4. **Define ACV vs RCV precisely; frame recoverable depreciation honestly.**
   **ACV (Actual Cash Value)** = replacement cost **minus depreciation** (for age, wear,
   condition). **RCV (Replacement Cost Value)** = the cost to repair/replace with materials
   of **like kind and quality WITHOUT** deducting depreciation, subject to policy limits.
   Under an RCV policy the insurer typically pays **in two stages**: a first ACV payment
   (minus deductible), then releases the held **recoverable depreciation** as a second
   payment **only after the work is completed and the final invoice/receipts are submitted**.
   Under an ACV-only settlement (or a roof-surface ACV endorsement), depreciation is
   **non-recoverable** — the homeowner absorbs both the deductible and the depreciation.
   Do NOT present an old/worn roof as if its full replacement cost will be paid.

5. **The recover/overlay prohibitions live in IRC Section R908** — specifically the
   three-condition "recover NOT allowed" list at **R908.3.1.1** (nested under R908.3.1 "Roof
   Re-cover"); cite **R908.3.1.1** for the conditions, never bare "R908.3" (the 2024 IRC
   renumbers). New Jersey adopts the IRC via the NJ Uniform Construction Code; the durable NJ
   citation is **N.J.A.C. 5:23-6.4** (Rehabilitation Subcode). A roof-over is PROHIBITED where:
   (a) the existing roof/covering is **water-soaked or deteriorated** (not an adequate base);
   (b) the existing covering is **slate, clay, cement, or asbestos-cement tile** — and NJ's
   5:23-6.4 **also adds wood shake**; or (c) the roof already has **two or more** applications
   of any covering (no third layer). After a leak, storm, or fire — where decking is wet,
   rotted, charred, or compromised — a recover is generally NOT code-compliant and a full
   tear-off is required. **Do NOT write "the model IRC bars roofing over wood shake"** — that
   conflates the two; for NJ cite **N.J.A.C. 5:23-6.4** (which includes wood shake).

6. **State any percentage wind/named-storm deductible AND any NJ "matching" requirement as
   POLICY/state-specific and time-sensitive — never as a guaranteed mandate.** Some NJ
   homeowners policies carry a **percentage** hurricane/named-storm/wind deductible (a percent
   of the Coverage A limit, not a flat dollar), and NJ is among the ~19 states + DC with such
   deductibles — but whether ANY individual policy carries one is policy-specific. There is
   **NO** statewide NJ "matching" / line-of-sight mandate (no statute or N.J.A.C. 11:2-17
   provision requires replacing undamaged adjacent roof/siding to match); whether your insurer
   pays to match undamaged sections is **policy-dependent**. Compliant: "Whether your insurer
   pays to match undamaged sections depends on your policy language, not a New Jersey requirement."

7. **Fire/lightning average claim = $88,170 (current), NOT $77,340.** The current Triple-I
   (2019–2023, ISO/Verisk) fire & lightning average claim is **$88,170**; the older ~$77,340
   figure (still in `facts-cost-stats.md` §8) is **superseded**. Use $88,170 or state
   qualitatively ("tens of thousands of dollars — far higher than a typical wind/hail claim").
   Confirm wind/hail frequency is **1 in 36** (~2.78%), water/freezing 1 in 67, fire/lightning
   1 in 430 (all current Triple-I). There is **NO** roof-only average claim payout from any
   named authority — never invent "a roof claim averages $X"; cite the peril-level figure and
   label it all-property, not roof-only.

8. **Post-fire: charred/heat-weakened framing + saturated decking are REPLACED after a
   structural assessment — a roofer cannot perform the structural sign-off.** A fire-damaged
   roof gets a formal post-fire structural assessment (often a licensed **structural
   engineer**); the char layer has essentially zero residual capacity and is removed, and the
   rebuild meets **current** codes. Fire-covering classes **Class A / B / C** are system
   ratings assigned by **UL 790 / ASTM E108** (cross-ref `facts-materials-economics.md` §5b);
   **untreated cedar is generally non-classified**, and a Class A wood-shake roof is an
   **assembly** rating, not the covering alone. Do NOT say smoke staining alone structurally
   weakens wood — it does not (the structural concern is char + the heat-affected zone, plus
   corrosion of metal connectors).

9. **Overlay is NOT equal to a tear-off — never present a roof-over as delivering a tear-off's
   benefit.** A recover (a) **hides** deck rot / water damage a tear-off would catch and repair;
   (b) traps heat/moisture and **cuts the new shingles' service life ~20–30%** (industry
   estimate, present as a range); (c) **telegraphs** the old shingle profile (shingles take the
   shape of what is under them — a smooth substrate is required); (d) adds **~2–4.5 lb/sq ft** of
   dead load (a second layer = thousands of extra pounds); and (e) forces a costlier future
   **double tear-off**. Present the cost savings (~20–25% / $2,000–$5,000, **national** figures)
   ONLY alongside these trade-offs — never as a free lunch. Do NOT say a recover "voids the
   manufacturer warranty" as a flat fact, and do NOT invent a warranty clause number.

10. **No NJ-specific / Essex-County cost figure is sourced here.** All overlay-vs-tear-off and
    replacement dollar figures are **national** aggregator data (HomeGuide/Angi) — label them
    national, or pull a NJ figure from `facts-materials-economics.md` §7 with its own named
    source. Do NOT assert a Newark/Essex overlay-vs-tear-off price.

## 1. Insurance claim economics — ACV, RCV, depreciation, deductibles

| Claim | Value | Named source | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Actual Cash Value (ACV)** | The cost to repair/replace damaged property based on its value considering age, wear, and depreciation — i.e., **replacement cost MINUS depreciation** (ACV = replacement cost − depreciation). An ACV policy pays to replace the home/possessions minus a deduction for depreciation. | NAIC; Insurance Information Institute (Triple-I) | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **Replacement Cost Value (RCV)** | The cost to repair/replace using materials of **like kind and quality WITHOUT** deducting for depreciation, subject to policy limits. An RCV policy pays the cost of rebuilding/repairing/replacing without a depreciation deduction. | NAIC; Insurance Information Institute (Triple-I) | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **Recoverable depreciation** | The gap between RCV and ACV (RCV − ACV) that an RCV policy pays back to the insured **after the work is completed and documented** (invoice/receipts provided). It exists **only** under replacement-cost coverage. | NAIC (replacement cost vs ACV guidance) | SECONDARY-named |
| **RCV pays in two stages** | The FIRST payment is typically based on ACV (minus deductible); the withheld recoverable depreciation is released as a **second payment only after the work is completed** and the final invoice/receipts are submitted. | Insurance Information Institute (Triple-I) — claims-payment process | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **Non-recoverable depreciation** | Depreciation the insured can never collect back — arises under an ACV-only policy or when a specific item (e.g., the roof surface under a roof-ACV endorsement) is settled on an ACV basis. Under ACV the amount due is simply ACV minus deductible; no completion-tied second payment. | NAIC; Insurance Information Institute (Triple-I) | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **Roof-surface ACV endorsement** | A policy otherwise written on a replacement-cost basis may contain a roof-surface ACV endorsement that settles the **roof covering on an ACV basis only**; depreciation on the roof is then non-recoverable even though the rest of the dwelling is RCV. Whether a roof is ACV or RCV can significantly change the payout, especially for older roofs. | Liberty Mutual (roofs and home insurance) | SECONDARY-named |
| **How roof depreciation is estimated** | Based on the property's condition when damaged, the cost of a new item, and the item's typical useful life — not arbitrary. For roofs it reflects age plus pre-existing wear (granule loss, curling, prior repairs); composition-shingle roofs are often assumed a ~20–30 year useful life. | NAIC; Insurance Information Institute (Triple-I) | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **Deductible mechanics** | The homeowner pays the policy deductible; it is subtracted **once** from the covered loss. Under an RCV two-stage payment the deductible is applied once, so ACV-payment + recoverable-depreciation = RCV − deductible. Deductible and depreciation are distinct. | Insurance Information Institute (Triple-I); NAIC | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **Guaranteed vs Extended Replacement Cost** | Guaranteed Replacement Cost pays whatever it costs to rebuild as-was, even above the policy limit; Extended Replacement Cost pays a defined percentage over the stated limit (commonly 20–25%). These matter mainly in large-scale/total-loss rebuilds, not typical partial roof claims. | Insurance Information Institute (Triple-I) | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **Percentage / named-storm / hurricane deductibles exist** | Some homeowners policies carry a separate **percentage** deductible (a percent of the dwelling Coverage A limit, not a flat dollar) for wind/hail or hurricane/named-storm losses — commonly ~1%–5% of insured value — which can produce large out-of-pocket amounts. | Insurance Information Institute (Triple-I); NAIC | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **New Jersey is among states with hurricane/named-storm deductibles** | Triple-I and NAIC list New Jersey among ~19 states + DC with a hurricane/named-storm deductible. *Time-sensitive (confirmed current to mid-2026); whether any INDIVIDUAL NJ policy carries one is policy-specific.* | Insurance Information Institute (Triple-I); NAIC | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **NJIUA hurricane deductible structure (NJ FAIR Plan)** | The NJ Insurance Underwriting Association Hurricane Deductible Program applies a **2%, 3%, or 4%** percentage deductible (on the greater of building or contents limit) triggered when the National Weather Service measures sustained winds **≥74 mph** anywhere in the state, within a window from 12 hours before to 12 hours after the last such measurement; it replaces the all-peril deductible when higher. | New Jersey Insurance Underwriting Association (NJIUA) Hurricane Deductible Program policyholder notice | PRIMARY |
| **NJ requires insurer notice of hurricane-deductible programs** | NJ administrative rules require insurers to give policyholders specific notice of their hurricane-deductible programs and how the deductible applies and interacts with other deductibles. | N.J.A.C. 11:2-42 (NJ DOBI windstorm/hurricane deductible notice rules) | PRIMARY |

- **Cross-ref:** peril-level claim frequency & average-claim figures (wind/hail 1 in 36, $14,747;
  water/freezing 1 in 67, $15,400; fire/lightning 1 in 430, **$88,170**) live in
  `facts-cost-stats.md` §8 — cite there, do not duplicate. **There is NO roof-only average payout**
  from any named authority (see Gaps).
- **Banned:** presenting an old/worn roof as if full replacement cost will be paid; calling the
  recoverable-depreciation second payment automatic (it is completion-and-invoice contingent and
  RCV-only); stating a percentage hurricane deductible applies to a specific policy as fact;
  interpreting the customer's specific policy.
- **Attributions:** "Under a replacement-cost (RCV) policy, the insurer commonly pays first on an
  actual-cash-value basis — replacement cost minus depreciation and your deductible — and releases
  the held 'recoverable depreciation' as a second payment after the roof is completed and invoiced.
  Under an actual-cash-value settlement, that depreciation is not recoverable. We explain these
  general terms; what your specific policy pays is between you and your insurer."

## 2. The claim PROCESS — adjusters, scope, supplement, appraisal, payment

| Claim | Value | Named source | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|

Both staff and independent adjusters work for the insurer. | Insurance Information Institute (Triple-I); NAIC State Licensing Handbook | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **Scope of loss** | A document/set of measurements describing the amount and type of damage plus the quantity, quality, and current cost of materials and labor needed to restore to pre-loss condition (including code-required upgrades). A normal part of larger claims; the insurer reviews the revised estimate, often with photos, code citations, and invoices. | Insurance Information Institute (Triple-I) — claims-process guidance | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **Appraisal clause + mechanics** | Most homeowners policies contain an appraisal clause to resolve disputes over the **AMOUNT** of loss (not coverage). If the parties cannot agree, either may demand appraisal: each side selects a competent independent appraiser, the two select a neutral umpire, and a written award signed by any two of the three sets the amount of loss. Each party pays its own appraiser and they split the umpire's cost. | **Prompt notice + proof of loss** | Policies require prompt notice of loss and commonly require a sworn **proof of loss** (a signed, itemized statement of the claimed loss) within a set period after the insurer requests it — **frequently 60 days**. *This 60-day figure is a common POLICY term, not a confirmed NJ statute; the deadline is contractual and can be modified/extended by state law and for good cause.* | United Policyholders (proof-of-loss guidance); NAIC Model Unfair Claims Settlement Practices Regulation | SECONDARY-named |

## 3. NJ public-adjuster compliance — the hard line (N.J.S.A. 17:22B / NJ DOBI)

## 7. Post-fire structural assessment & fire classification (UL 790 / ASTM E108)

| Claim | Value | Named source | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Roof fire classes set by UL 790 / ASTM E108** | Class A, B, C are assigned by **UL 790** and **ASTM E108** "Standard Test Methods for Fire Tests of Roof Coverings," via a spread-of-flame test, an intermittent-flame test, and a burning-brand test for exterior fire exposure. **Class A = highest** fire resistance (severe exposure), **Class B = moderate**, **Class C = light/lowest.** *Cite by name/number; no edition year (both paywalled).* | UL 790; ASTM E108 | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **IRC fire-classification code section** | **IRC Chapter 9 "Roof Assemblies," Section R902 "Fire classification" (R902.1)** requires roof assemblies to be classified Class A, B, C, or nonclassified per ASTM E108 or UL 790. The 2024 IRC clarifies the rating applies to the entire roof **ASSEMBLY** (covering + underlayment + deck) and lists certain assemblies (masonry, exposed concrete decks, ferrous/copper shingles, qualifying clay/concrete tile on noncombustible decks, listed slate over underlayment) as Class A by definition. | ICC IRC R902 / Chapter 9 (2018/2021/2024) | PRIMARY |
| **Cedar shake fire classification** | Untreated/natural cedar shakes and shingles are generally **non-fire-classified** on their own. Fire-retardant-treated (FRT) wood can achieve Class B or C. A **Class A wood-shake roof is achieved only as a tested ASSEMBLY** (wood over a listed fire-barrier/underlayment on specified sheathing), not from the wood covering alone. *Cross-ref `facts-materials-economics.md` §5b.* | UL/ICC-ES listed roof-system documentation; wood-roofing manufacturer technical bulletins (restating UL 790 / ASTM E108) | SECONDARY-named |
| **A roof is a structural ASSEMBLY, not just a covering** | A roof assembly includes covering, underlayment, sheathing/decking, and structural framing (rafters/trusses). Fire damage must be assessed across the whole assembly, not just the visible covering. | U.S. Forest Products Laboratory (FPL); EDT Engineers | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **Fire/heat degrades wood framing and decking** | Fire converts the outer wood to a **char layer (~300 °C+) with essentially ZERO residual structural capacity** that must be removed; beneath it a heat-affected zone retains only ~85–90% of original strength/stiffness. Loss of cross-section in 2x rafters/trusses raises stresses and can drop members below safe capacity. Plywood/OSB sheathing chars, loses thickness/stiffness, can delaminate, and combined with water saturation can become spongy or sag — a collapse warning, not to be walked on. | U.S. Forest Products Laboratory (FPL); American Wood Council (AWC) Technical Report 10; Fire Engineering | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **Wood char rate (named figure)** | For structural fire design under standard fire exposure, the American Wood Council uses a nominal one-dimensional char rate of **1.5 inches of wood per hour (βn = 1.5 in/hr)**. For residual-capacity calculations an "effective char depth" (nominal char increased ~20%) accounts for the weakened heat-affected zone; the char layer is assigned zero strength. | American Wood Council (AWC) National Design Specification (NDS) / Fire Design Specification (FDS) and Technical Report 10; WoodWorks | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **Field thresholds for charred members** | Field rules of thumb (not code): char up to ~6 mm (0.2 in) or up to ~10% of a truss member's cross-section may sometimes be tolerable subject to engineering judgment; charred material has no residual capacity and must be removed; any significant char/surface loss should be evaluated by a structural engineer or building official. | U.S. Forest Products Laboratory (FPL post-fire assessment manual); InterNACHI | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **Fire weakens metal connectors, truss plates, fasteners** | Structural-steel strength loss begins ~300 °C and accelerates above 400 °C; by ~550–600 °C steel may retain only ~40–60% of yield strength. On wood trusses, heat/char beneath metal connector (gusset) plates reduces plate-tooth embedment, compromising the connection even if plates look intact; heat-treated bolts lose temper; rubber/neoprene washers on metal panels melt and create leak paths. Affected fasteners/connectors are often replaced. | Steel Construction Institute; EDT Engineers; U.S. Forest Products Laboratory | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **Firefighting water saturates the assembly + mold risk** | Extinguishing water saturates decking, insulation, and framing, causing further structural degradation and elevated mold/decay risk; smoke residue plus moisture accelerates corrosion of metal components. Prompt drying and assessment are emphasized. | U.S. Forest Products Laboratory; ANSI/IICRC S700 | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **Smoke/soot residue is acidic/corrosive (but doesn't structurally weaken wood)** | Smoke/soot residues are acidic and can keep damaging metals, electronics, and electrical insulation after the fire if not promptly cleaned; with moisture their corrosivity rises sharply. **Wood load capacity is NOT reduced by smoke staining alone**, but soot must be removed from members kept in service. Ash/soot may contain heavy metals (lead, arsenic). | ANSI/IICRC S700; U.S. Forest Products Laboratory; public-health/restoration guidance | PRIMARY-attrib |
| **Fire-damaged framing/decking is replaced after a structural assessment — not roofed over** | A fire-damaged roof should get a formal post-fire structural assessment — often by a licensed structural engineer — before reconstruction; temporary shoring may be needed and the rebuild must meet **current** codes, not merely replicate pre-fire conditions. Charred/heat-compromised rafters, trusses, and sheathing are typically removed and **REPLACED, not roofed over.** NRCA advises homeowners not to DIY (safety + preserving evidence for the adjuster), to tarp openings, and to engage a credentialed roofing professional. | EDT Engineers / structural-engineering practice; NFPA 921; NRCA ("Roof Repair After a Fire"); InterNACHI; FEMA | PRIMARY-attrib |

- **Cross-ref:** cedar fire ratings + the Class A/B/C system are also in
  `facts-materials-economics.md` §5b. Fire/lightning claim frequency/severity ($88,170) →
  `facts-cost-stats.md` §8.
- **Banned:** implying NQR performs the post-fire structural sign-off (a licensed structural engineer
  does; NQR roofs to the engineer's/code's requirements); stating smoke staining alone structurally
  weakens wood (it does not — char + heat-affected zone + metal corrosion are the structural concerns);
  asserting a recover/overlay over fire-charred or water-saturated decking (a tear-off is required).
- **Attributions:** "After a fire, a roof is a whole assembly — covering, deck, and framing. Charred wood
  has essentially no remaining strength and is removed; saturated decking and heat-weakened rafters or
  trusses are replaced, and the rebuild meets current code. A licensed structural engineer assesses the
  framing; we perform the roofing to that assessment and code."
